**Short answer:** a **theoretical framework** is an existing theory you borrow as a lens; a **conceptual framework** is your own map of how the key concepts in *your* study connect; an **analytical framework** is how you will turn your data into findings. A **literature review** is none of these — it reports what earlier studies found and shows the gap your study fills.

If you've read five definitions and they all contradict each other, you're not imagining it. Different fields use these terms loosely, and many supervisors use them interchangeably in feedback. Below is the version that works across the social sciences, education, business and communication — followed by one study walked through all four, so you can see how they fit together.

## The difference at a glance

| | Literature review | Theoretical framework | Conceptual framework | Analytical framework |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | A synthesis of what other studies found | An established theory used as a lens | Your own model of the concepts in your study and how they relate | Your plan for turning data into findings |
| Question it answers | What is already known, and what's missing? | Through what lens do I look at this? | What exactly am I studying, and how do the parts connect? | How will I analyse what I collect? |
| Whose is it | Other researchers' | A theorist's (e.g. Blau, Bourdieu, Rogers) | Yours | Yours, built on a method |
| Typical form | Thematic narrative with citations | 1–3 pages explaining the theory and why it fits | A diagram + a short explanation | Coding scheme, categories, analysis steps |
| Where it usually sits | Chapter 2 | Chapter 2 or its own section | End of chapter 2 or start of chapter 3 | Methodology chapter |

## Theoretical framework: the lens

A theoretical framework is an **existing, published theory** that tells you what to pay attention to. Grant and Osanloo (2014) describe it as the blueprint for the whole dissertation — it shapes your research question, what you measure or ask about, and how you interpret results.

The test: *if you removed the theory, would your interview questions or variables change?* If not, the theory is decoration, not a framework.

## Conceptual framework: your map

A conceptual framework is **your own** argument about the key concepts in your study and the relationships you expect between them. Miles and Huberman (1994) define it as a visual or written explanation of the main things to be studied and their presumed relationships. Ravitch and Riggan (2017) go further: it's the argument for why your study matters and why your design makes sense.

It often draws on one or more theories — but it is specific to *your* case, your context and your variables.

## Analytical framework: from data to findings

An analytical framework is the structure you use to **analyse** your data: the categories, codes or variables you look for, and the steps you follow. In qualitative research this is often a coding frame — for example, deductive codes taken from your theory plus inductive codes that emerge from the data, analysed with thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006).

If your theoretical framework tells you *what to look at*, your analytical framework tells you *how you'll know when you've seen it*.

## Literature review: not a framework at all

The literature review summarises and critiques what previous studies found. Its job is to show the **gap**. The most common mistake we see is a "theoretical framework" section that is really a second literature review — a list of studies on the topic, with no theory doing any work.

## One study, all four

Topic: **How does remote work affect trust in project teams?** (an illustrative example)

| | What it looks like in this study |
|---|---|
| Literature review | Studies on remote teams show mixed results for trust; most measure it with surveys in large tech companies; few look at small teams or explain *how* trust builds remotely. **Gap:** the mechanism. |
| Theoretical framework | **Social Exchange Theory** (Blau, 1964): trust grows through repeated, reciprocal exchanges where people feel obligations are met. Lens: look at exchanges, reciprocity and perceived fairness. |
| Conceptual framework | Remote work intensity → fewer informal exchanges → less visible reciprocity → lower trust; *moderated by* how long the team has worked together. Drawn as a diagram with these four boxes. |
| Analytical framework | Semi-structured interviews; thematic analysis with deductive codes from Social Exchange Theory (reciprocity, obligation, fairness) and inductive codes for anything new; compare teams with short vs long tenure. |

Notice the chain: the **gap** (mechanism) justifies the **theory** (exchange explains mechanisms), the theory gives the **concepts** in your map, and those concepts become the **codes** in your analysis. When one link is missing — say, codes that have nothing to do with the theory — that's when a supervisor writes "this doesn't hang together".

## Do I need all four?

Most theses need a literature review and a clear analytical approach. Whether you need a separate theoretical *and* conceptual framework depends on your field and programme — check your template or ask your supervisor. What every study needs is the **alignment**: the question, the lens, the concepts and the analysis pointing in the same direction.

If you're still choosing the lens, read [how to choose a theoretical framework for your dissertation](/blog/how-to-choose-a-theoretical-framework).
