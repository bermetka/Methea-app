**Short answer:** a research question becomes narrow enough when it names **who** you are studying, **where and when**, **one phenomenon** you want to understand, and a **question word that matches your method** — and when you can realistically collect the data before your deadline. Most broad questions fail on the third point: they name a whole field ("the impact of social media on students") instead of one thing happening in it.

Almost every supervisor's first comment on a proposal is some version of "this is too broad". It's frustrating because it doesn't tell you *which way* to narrow. Below are five moves that do, a before-and-after table, and a quick test you can run on your own question.

## Signs your research question is too broad

- You could write a book on it — or several already exist.
- It contains a whole field as a noun: "education", "technology", "leadership", "mental health".
- It asks about "the impact of X on Y" with no mechanism, group or setting.
- You can't say which people you would interview or which data you would download.
- Your literature search returns tens of thousands of results and none of them feel like *your* study.

If two or more of these are true, narrow before you write anything else. Every later chapter — the framework, the method, the interview guide — inherits the vagueness of the question.

## Five moves to narrow a research question

1. **Name the people.** Replace "students" with a group you can actually reach: "first-year international Master's students", "nurses on night shifts", "founders of food delivery start-ups".
2. **Fix the place and the time.** One country, one city, one organisation, one programme; a period you can describe ("since the 2023 policy change", "during their first semester").
3. **Pick one phenomenon, not a field.** Ask what exactly happens. "Social media and wellbeing" becomes "comparing oneself to peers on Instagram"; "leadership" becomes "how team leads give feedback remotely".
4. **Choose the question word on purpose.** *How* and *why* questions point to qualitative designs (interviews, case studies). *To what extent*, *how much* and *is there a relationship* point to quantitative designs (surveys, secondary data). Mixing them in one sentence is a common sign of a question that hasn't decided what it is.
5. **Check it against your deadline.** Can you get access to these people, collect this data and analyse it in the time you have? If not, narrow the group or the setting again — not the depth.

Booth and colleagues (2016) offer a useful three-part check: *I am studying ___, because I want to find out ___, in order to help my reader understand ___.* If you can't fill the last blank, the question may be narrow but not yet significant.

## Before and after: three examples

| | Too broad | Narrowed |
|---|---|---|
| Education | What is the impact of AI on education? | How do first-year Master's students at one university decide when using ChatGPT for coursework is acceptable? |
| Business | How does remote work affect employees? | How do members of small, fully remote software teams build trust with colleagues they have never met in person? |
| Communication | How does social media influence young people? | How do Instagram users aged 18–24 in Almaty describe comparing themselves with peers, and how does it affect what they post? |

These are illustrative examples. Notice what changed each time: a specific group, a specific setting, one phenomenon and a *how* question that fits interviews. None of the narrowed versions is "smaller" in importance — they are just answerable.

## Gap-spotting vs questioning assumptions

Most students narrow by **gap-spotting**: finding something earlier studies haven't covered ("no one has studied this in Kyrgyzstan"). That works, but Alvesson and Sandberg (2011) argue that the more interesting questions often come from **problematization** — challenging an assumption the existing literature takes for granted. Instead of "no one has studied remote trust in small teams", ask "the literature assumes trust needs face-to-face contact; what if small remote teams build it differently?"

You don't need to do this for every thesis. But if your supervisor says the question is narrow yet "not interesting", this is usually the fix.

## Expect to narrow more than once

Agee (2009) describes developing qualitative research questions as a reflective process: questions are refined as you read, plan and even collect data. Your first narrowed version is a working question. It will likely sharpen again once you choose a theoretical framework — the theory tells you which part of the phenomenon to look at.
