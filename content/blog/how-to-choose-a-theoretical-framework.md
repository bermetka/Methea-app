**Short answer:** choose a theoretical framework by starting from the **key concepts in your research question**, shortlisting theories that explain how those concepts relate, and keeping the one that passes four fit tests: it covers your concepts, works at the **level** you study (people, organisations or societies), does the **job** your question needs (explain, predict or interpret), and can be tested with **data you can actually collect** before your deadline. Then check how the theory has been critiqued, and write down why you chose it over the alternatives.

Picking a theory feels like a test of how much you've read. It isn't. It's a matching exercise between your question and a small number of candidate theories — and the matching can be done step by step.

## Step 1: Pull the concepts out of your question

Grant and Osanloo (2014) describe the theoretical framework as the blueprint for the whole dissertation, so it has to be built around your problem, not chosen first and fitted later. Underline the nouns and verbs in your research question that carry the meaning.

> How do members of small, fully remote software teams **build trust** with **colleagues** they have never met?

The concepts here are *trust*, *building* it over time, and the *relationship between colleagues*. A useful theory must say something about at least these.

## Step 2: Shortlist theories that explain those concepts

Look at the five to ten most-cited studies on your topic and note which theories they use. You'll usually see the same two or three come up. Add any theory your supervisor or programme expects, then stop — a shortlist of three is enough.

Here is how some widely used theories map to common question types:

| If your question is about… | Theories often used | Original source |
|---|---|---|
| Whether people adopt a new technology | Technology Acceptance Model | Davis (1989) |
| How an innovation spreads through a group or society | Diffusion of Innovations | Rogers (1962) |
| Trust, reciprocity and relationships | Social Exchange Theory | Blau (1964) |
| What motivates people from the inside vs the outside | Self-Determination Theory | Deci & Ryan (1985) |
| Why people intend to do something, and whether they do it | Theory of Planned Behaviour | Ajzen (1991) |

For the remote-trust question, Social Exchange Theory is a strong candidate: it explains trust as something that grows through repeated, reciprocal exchanges.

## Step 3: Run four fit tests

| Test | Ask yourself | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Concept fit | Does the theory name and explain my key concepts? | You have to stretch the theory's terms to cover your topic |
| Level fit | Does it work at the level I study — individuals, teams, organisations, societies? | An individual-level theory used to explain national policy |
| Job fit | Do I need to *explain* a mechanism, *predict* an outcome or *interpret* meaning — and is this theory built for that? | A predictive model in a purely interpretive interview study |
| Data and timeline fit | Can I collect the data the theory needs before my deadline? | The theory is about change over years; you have three months |

The last test is the one most often skipped. A theory about how adoption unfolds over time needs data over time. If you can only interview people once, either pick a different theory or narrow your question to a part of the process you *can* observe.

## Step 4: Check the critiques

Every established theory has known limits. Sutton and Staw (1995) remind us that references, data and diagrams on their own are not theory — a theory explains *why*. Read at least one critique or update of your chosen theory and decide what you'll do about it: acknowledge it, combine the theory with another, or limit your claims. Examiners notice when a framework is presented as if no one had ever questioned it.

## Step 5: Write down why — in your own words

Your justification should answer three questions:

1. Why does this theory fit *my* question better than the alternatives I considered?
2. Which of its concepts will shape my interview questions, variables or codes?
3. What are its limits, and how will I handle them?

Collins and Stockton (2018) show that in qualitative research the theory should shape the whole study — from the questions you ask to how you analyse. If your answer to question 2 is "none", the theory is decoration. Kivunja (2018) is a useful read if you're unsure how the theoretical framework differs from the conceptual framework you'll build next — or see our guide to [theoretical vs conceptual vs analytical frameworks](/blog/theoretical-vs-conceptual-vs-analytical-framework).

## Using more than one theory

Two theories can work when each does a separate job — for example, one explains the mechanism and another explains the context. Two theories that explain the same thing usually weaken the framework, because it's no longer clear which one your findings speak to.
